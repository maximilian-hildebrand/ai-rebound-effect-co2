\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Artificial intelligence is increasingly positioned as a key 
enabler of sustainable development. Proponents argue that 
AI-driven optimization of energy grids, supply chains, and 
industrial processes offers a pathway to significant reductions 
in greenhouse gas emissions \cite{mhlanga2025}. This optimistic 
narrative, however, stands in tension with a growing body of 
evidence suggesting that the environmental costs of AI 
deployment are both larger and more complex than commonly 
assumed \cite{luccioni2025}.

The central difficulty lies not in the efficiency gains 
themselves, which are real and measurable, but in the systemic 
responses they provoke. Lower costs of computation encourage 
greater usage; more efficient logistics enable higher order 
volumes; cheaper production expands consumption. These dynamics 
are not incidental but follow a well-established economic 
principle: the Jevons Paradox, which holds that efficiency 
improvements tend to increase rather than reduce overall 
resource consumption by stimulating demand. Applied to AI, 
this principle raises a fundamental question about whether 
technological progress alone can deliver the emissions 
reductions it promises.

This paper examines to what extent rebound effects at the 
direct and indirect level counteract the ecological efficiency 
gains of AI deployment and thereby contribute to a 
deterioration of the global CO\textsubscript{2} balance. 
The analysis proceeds as follows. Section 2 introduces the 
theoretical framework of the Jevons Paradox and the rebound 
effect. Section 3 examines direct rebound effects arising 
from AI infrastructure. Section 4 analyses indirect rebound 
effects across connected economic sectors. Section 5 
discusses the limitations of the argument and its policy 
implications. Section 6 concludes.
